1.6Signals and Working Hypotheses

Signals and Working Hypotheses

Revenue decline of 10% (2023 to 2024):

  • client loss (churn increased);
  • weak new-client acquisition from the sales funnel (win rate declined);
  • a possible contributing factor: a longer sales cycle.

EBITDA margin of 3.4%: means that roughly 96% of revenue is consumed by costs (COGS + OPEX), i.e., the business operates close to break-even. Risk: any cost increase pushes the company into a loss.

Working assumption: the products generate no revenue but are funded through OPEX, i.e., OPEX includes development costs (salaries, tooling, maintenance…). Excluding the products, EBITDA could be higher; once product investment is factored in, the margin sits near zero.

Problem Statement

The Core Problem at Proscom

The company is in a state of structural deadlock. It has built a product portfolio it cannot sell, which has become a toxic drag on the business. Custom development (the services business) can no longer fund this drag because its own efficiency has declined, while the products cannot sustain themselves.

The trap closes: saving the products requires scaling the services business several times over; but scaling services requires withdrawing all resources and management focus from the products, which would mean their immediate shutdown and the recognition of unrecoverable losses.

Root Causes and Consequences (hypotheses)

Product Deadlock

The root of the product problem is not merely weak sales but a systemic gap between the studio's core competencies and the requirements of the product market:

  • UI/UX expertise: as a strong design studio, Proscom built products that look excellent (polished interface, MVP rituals) but lack unique technological or market value.
  • Imitation instead of innovation: an enterprise messenger, a low-code platform, an LMS and AI scoring are four highly complex, technologically distinct and fiercely competitive markets. Building four clones of existing solutions without a breakthrough differentiator meant weak demand from the outset.
  • No distribution capability: the company knows how to sell its specialists' hours to large clients (B2G/Enterprise), but has no competencies, processes or infrastructure for marketing and selling B2B products (SaaS/licenses).

Financial Deadlock

Funding a large-scale R&D cycle for four platforms from a small studio's working capital created an unsustainable financial structure:

  • Capital mismatch: developing enterprise products requires long-term, high-risk venture capital (multi-million investments with a 3–5 year payback horizon). The studio tried to fund it from project cash flow (short-cycle government contract revenue), which is highly volatile and exposed to cash gaps.
  • Erosion of working capital: the products became a "black hole" for the services business's net profit. Instead of reinvesting design-studio profits into growing the studio itself, the company irrevocably burned them on product developers' salaries.

Operational Deadlock

As the products consumed ever more resources, the company's core cash cow, the services business, was left without proper management:

  • Loss of operational control: the founders' attention was absorbed by the product startups. As a result, control over staff utilization (utilization rate) and contract profitability weakened in the services business. Revenue per employee fell, driving a 10% decline in total revenue and compressing net margin to a token 3.4%.
  • Vicious circle of dependency: the studio can no longer grow because the products drain its resources and management focus. Yet the products cannot develop because the studio earns less and can no longer fund them.

THE PARADOX

  1. The products must be saved through investment.
  2. But there is no capital to invest.
  3. Capital is available only if the services business is restored.
  4. But the services business can be restored only by withdrawing resources from the products.
  5. Withdraw the resources, and the products die.

This is a vicious circle with no obvious way out.

3

Strategic Choice and Direction

Decision Logic

A choice must be made.

The shareholders chose: services (per the job description).

Why? Because:

The services business generates cash TODAY (RUB 226M in revenue).

The products generate ZERO (apart from costs).

Restoring the services margin from 3.4% to 20% yields RUB 45M in profit instead of RUB 7.7M.

That cash can later be reinvested in the products. Attempting to save both at once would break both.

Strategic Direction

First 18 months:

  • Shut down or mothball the products (Violet, Tada, Nanimator, Vector)
  • Restore the services margin from 3.4% to 20%
  • Stop the cash bleed

Months 18–36:

  • Grow the services business organically and through M&A
  • Revisit the products later (if at all)

Key Deliverable of the Phase: the Vendor Management System

1. Building the Partner Network

We do not recruit companies off the street via ads. Building a pool of 10+ reliable studios follows a three-stage filter:

Rigorous accreditation (Security + Legal)

Verification of the agency's financial stability. No shell companies. Mandatory mirror NDA with penalties for leaks of Enterprise clients' data.

Technical scoring

Before giving a partner a real assignment, we take 2–3 of their developers and put them through our internal assessment. We must verify firsthand that their "Seniors" are genuinely Senior, not yesterday's interns.

Vendor Directory

Create an internal database in which all studios are categorized by technology and grade, with prices fixed for one year.

2. Team Staffing Process (Vendor Management)

The Vendor Manager receives a request from Sales, breaks it down into the required grades and books ready-to-deploy specialists from the pool of vetted partners at our fixed rate card, with a final screening by our team leads.

3. Partner Performance Control (operational and management filter)

Real-time control (single task tracker)

External developers work strictly within our system (Jira / Yandex Tracker). No end-of-month "we did something" reports. We see every task: who is working on it, how many hours have been spent and at which stage it is currently stalled.

Regular sync meetings (PM-to-PM Sync)

Weekly / daily: we reconcile hours, review the milestone schedule and promptly reallocate people if someone is underperforming.

Monthly rating (Vendor Health Check)

Each month the Vendor Manager compiles a report on all partners and scores them on three criteria: speed, defect count and quality of communication. Top-scoring studios receive the largest shares of new billion-ruble contracts. Studios that fall into the "red zone" are automatically removed from the pipeline and replaced by other companies from our pool.

Establishing a VMO (Vendor Management Office) within HR

Transform the recruiting function into a partner pool management function. HR becomes the owner of external resource quality, responsible for vetting, onboarding, assessing and rotating personnel within partner studios (70% of revenue in the integrator model).

Introduce the Vendor Manager role within the HR department. This is the bridge between Delivery (projects) and the external studios:

  • Sourcing new contractors, HR Due Diligence, quality control of onboarding and compliance with staffing SLAs
  • The Vendor Manager receives stack and timeline requirements from Sales/Delivery and routes them to the partner network
  • Implement the Vendor Performance Index (VPI)
  • Process standardization: communicates our internal Enterprise development standards
  • VPI (Vendor Performance Index) ≥ 4.5/5.0
  • Time-to-Submission ≤ 5 business days
  • Vendor Compliance: 100% of external specialists pass our internal technical assessment
  • Utilization Rate of external resources ≥ 85%

M&A and Post-Merger Integration (PMI)

HR support for M&A transactions. The goal is not merely to buy a studio but to integrate its people into our ecosystem without losing expertise.

  • HR Due Diligence: cultural and technical audit of teams before deal closing
  • Retention plan: locking in key talent for 12–18 months after deal closing
  • Aligning the salary grids and KPIs of acquired studios with our standards
  • Key talent Retention Rate ≥ 90% within 12 months after M&A
  • Time-to-Integration (aligning the acquired studio's management processes with the parent company) ≤ 90 days
  • eNPS of acquired teams ≥ 7.0 six months after integration

Retaining the Core Team

Retain the small core group (top architects, the delivery director, key tech leads) who manage external contractors.

  • Create an Expert Community program: develop internal architects as mentors for external partners
  • Performance-based Bonus system: reward key employees for ecosystem success rather than hours worked
  • Core team Attrition Rate ≤ 8% per year
  • Core team eNPS ≥ 8.5
  • Succession plan: 100% of critical roles have a succession plan

External Tech Employer Branding

We are building the company's brand as a technology hub. We attract strong leads and architects not by promising interesting coding work but by offering the status of expert-managers who set the rules of the game for the contractor market.

  • HR builds the EVP (employee value proposition) around expert influence in the AI integration market rather than around hiring itself
  • HR rewrites the role profile: we are looking not for coders but for Auditors & Mentors, with experience in conducting reviews, vendor management skills and the ability to reject substandard work with well-reasoned arguments
  • Time-to-Hire ≤ 50 days
  • Inbound Lead Quality ≥ 60%
  • Conversion Rate (Lead to Interview) ≥ 35%
  • Offer Acceptance Rate ≥ 75%
